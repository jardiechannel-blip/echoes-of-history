In the winter of 218 BC, a Carthaginian general led an army towards the Alps. His destination was Italy. His enemy was Rome. His name was Hannibal.

What followed would become one of the most extraordinary military campaigns in history. An army crossing mountains that seemed impossible to cross. Battles that shocked the Roman world. And a commander who came closer than almost anyone to bringing the Roman Republic to its knees.

But who was Hannibal? And why did Rome fear him so deeply?

To understand his story, we must go back to the rivalry between two great powers of the ancient Mediterranean.

Carthage was a wealthy and powerful city in North Africa, built on trade, commerce, and naval strength. Rome, meanwhile, was expanding across the Italian peninsula. Their ambitions eventually brought them into conflict.

The First Punic War ended in 241 BC with a Roman victory. Carthage lost control of Sicily and was forced to accept harsh financial conditions.

For Carthage, the defeat was humiliating. For Rome, it was the beginning of a much larger ambition.

Hannibal was born around 247 BC. His father, Hamilcar Barca, had been one of Carthage’s leading generals during the First Punic War. After the defeat, Hamilcar turned his attention towards Iberia, modern-day Spain, where he began building a new Carthaginian power base.

According to the ancient historian Polybius, Hamilcar made his young son swear an oath of eternal hostility towards Rome.

Whether the story happened exactly as described is uncertain. But there is little doubt that Hannibal grew up in a world shaped by war, rivalry, and resentment towards Rome.

As an adult, Hannibal took command of Carthaginian forces in Iberia. He expanded Carthaginian influence and strengthened his army.

Then, in 219 BC, he attacked the city of Saguntum, an ally of Rome.

Rome demanded that Carthage surrender Hannibal. Carthage refused.

The Second Punic War had begun.

Rome expected the conflict to take place largely in Spain or North Africa. Hannibal had a very different plan.

Instead of waiting for the Romans to attack, he decided to bring the war directly into Italy.

In 218 BC, Hannibal began one of the most daring military operations of the ancient world.

He marched his army through the Pyrenees, crossed the Rhône River, and continued towards the Alps.

His forces included infantry, cavalry, and war elephants. The exact size of the army is debated by historians, but the journey was extraordinarily difficult.

The Alps presented enormous obstacles. Narrow mountain paths, freezing temperatures, hostile tribes, and dangerous terrain threatened the entire expedition.

Men and animals died along the way. Supplies became scarce. The army was exhausted.

Yet Hannibal continued.

When he finally descended into northern Italy, he had lost a significant part of his original force. But he had achieved something Rome had not expected.

He had brought a Carthaginian army into the heart of Italy.

The Romans were forced to react.

Hannibal’s first major victories came at the River Trebia in 218 BC and Lake Trasimene in 217 BC.

At Trebia, he used the weather, the terrain, and a hidden force to trap the Roman army.

At Lake Trasimene, he prepared an ambush in a narrow valley beside the lake. The Roman forces marched straight into it.

Thousands of Roman soldiers were killed or captured.

Rome was now facing a terrifying enemy.

But Hannibal’s greatest victory was still to come.

After the defeat at Lake Trasimene, Rome appointed Quintus Fabius Maximus as dictator.

Fabius understood that fighting Hannibal directly was extremely dangerous. Instead, he avoided major battles, harassed Carthaginian forces, and tried to weaken them over time.

This strategy became known as the Fabian strategy.

Many Romans considered it cowardly. They wanted a decisive victory.

In 216 BC, Rome assembled one of the largest armies it had ever fielded and marched towards Hannibal.

The two armies met at Cannae, in southern Italy.

Hannibal had fewer soldiers, but he understood how to use them.

He positioned his infantry in a curved formation, with his centre deliberately placed forward. As the Romans advanced, the Carthaginian centre gradually gave ground.

At the same time, Hannibal’s cavalry defeated the Roman cavalry on the flanks.

Then the Carthaginian infantry on both sides turned inward, surrounding the Roman army.

The Romans were trapped.

What followed was a massacre.

Ancient sources report enormous Roman losses, although the exact numbers remain debated. The defeat was one of the worst disasters in Roman military history.

Hannibal had demonstrated that a smaller army could destroy a much larger force through superior planning, discipline, and battlefield tactics.

Several Roman allies defected to Carthage, including the important city of Capua.

For a moment, Rome seemed vulnerable.

But Hannibal had a problem.

He had won extraordinary victories, yet he had not destroyed Rome itself.

Why didn’t he march directly on the city?

Historians continue to debate this question.

Hannibal lacked the heavy siege equipment and reinforcements needed to capture a heavily defended city like Rome. His army was also operating far from its main supply bases.

His strategy depended on weakening Rome by breaking apart its network of Italian allies.

He hoped that enough cities would abandon Rome to force the Republic to negotiate.

But Rome refused to surrender.

The Roman Senate continued the war. Instead of accepting defeat, Rome adapted.

It avoided giving Hannibal another opportunity for a decisive victory. Roman forces attacked Carthaginian allies and gradually weakened his position in Italy.

Meanwhile, a young Roman commander named Publius Cornelius Scipio began to change the course of the war.

Scipio campaigned successfully in Spain and eventually took the war to North Africa.

Carthage was now under direct threat.

In 203 BC, Hannibal was recalled from Italy to defend his homeland.

After more than fifteen years of campaigning, the general who had once terrified Rome was forced to leave the Italian peninsula.

The final confrontation came at Zama in 202 BC.

Hannibal faced Scipio in a battle that would decide the future of the Mediterranean.

This time, Rome emerged victorious.

Carthage was defeated and forced to accept severe peace conditions. Its military power was broken, and Rome became the dominant power in the western Mediterranean.

Hannibal survived the defeat and later became involved in Carthaginian politics. But Rome continued to view him as a threat.

He eventually went into exile, moving between eastern kingdoms while avoiding Roman attempts to capture him.

Around 183 BC, Hannibal died, traditionally said to have taken poison rather than fall into Roman hands.

His death did not end the rivalry between Rome and Carthage.

In 146 BC, Rome destroyed Carthage during the Third Punic War.

The city that had once challenged Roman power was erased.

Hannibal never conquered Rome. He never destroyed the Republic. And in the end, Carthage itself was defeated.

Yet his legacy survived.

His crossing of the Alps, his victories at Trebia, Trasimene, and Cannae, and his ability to outthink larger armies made him one of the most studied commanders in military history.

He showed Rome that it could be defeated.

And perhaps that was his greatest achievement.

Hannibal did not destroy Rome.

But for years, he made the most powerful republic in the ancient world believe that it might not survive.
